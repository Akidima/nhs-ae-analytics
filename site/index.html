01 · What is this?

Someone kept a receipt
for every A&E visit in England.

Since 2015, NHS England has published one spreadsheet each month. Every hospital and walk-in centre in the country reports the same simple set of numbers: how many people walked through the door, how long they waited, and how many were admitted as emergencies.

This website reads those receipts so you don't have to. Everything here comes from one cleaned table of 28,212 monthly reports — after removing duplicates, fixing known errors, and reconciling against NHS national totals.

🚶

Attendances

People who arrived at an A&E or urgent care centre. Any arrival counts — by ambulance, by taxi, or on foot.

The three kinds of front door

Type 1 departments carry almost two-thirds of all demand — and nearly all of the waiting problem. In the last twelve months, only about 61% of Type 1 arrivals met the 4-hour standard, while walk-in centres managed 96.9%. This may suggest the queue isn't really about minor injuries — it's concentrated at the big hospital front doors.

02 · What does the data show?

One line tells the whole story.

Each dot below is one month of English A&E. The dashed gold line is the national promise: 95% of patients seen within four hours. Watch where the line goes.

How often did England keep its 4-hour promise?
Share of A&E patients seen within four hours, nationally, month by month. Hover any point for that month's numbers.
monthly performance 95% target
⚠ In eleven years, exactly  one  month met the 95% promise — July 2015. There hasn't been another since.

What changed: England came close year after year — 90.8% in June 2018, mid-80s in good winters — but never crossed the line again. Then the floor gave way: performance bottomed at 69.0% in November 2022, meaning nearly one patient in three waited more than four hours. The slow climb back to ~75% is real, but it remains far from the old normal. This may suggest something structural changed in 2021–22, not just a bad winter.

The same NHS, two different worlds.

In winter 2021–22, monthly performance at big consultant-led A&Es fell as low as  58.7%  — four patients in ten waited more than four hours.

Why this matters: if the whole system had simply slowed down, both lines would fall together. Instead, walk-in centres stayed above 94% the entire time while Type 1 dipped below 60%. This may suggest the pressure lives at the hospital door — emergencies, ambulance arrivals, and beds — not in the queues for plasters and stitches.

04 · The hidden number

The wait nobody talks about:
after the decision to admit.

Here is the part of A&E that rarely makes headlines. When doctors decide a patient must be admitted to a ward, the clock keeps running. Some patients wait half a day or more in A&E because no ward bed exists. These are called “trolley waits”.

05 · Rhythm of the year

England's A&E is busiest in July.
It copes worst in January.

Two rhythms hide inside the year. Attendance rises gently into summer — school holidays, festivals, injuries outdoors. Performance, though, sags in deep winter, when flu meets full wards.

Demand peaks in summer. Care quality dips in winter.
Average across 11 years. Bars: how busy each month is vs a typical month (100 = average). Line: share seen within 4 hours.
busyness vs average % seen within 4h
July The busiest month of the year — 5% busier than average. Summer pressure is real, but hospitals absorb it.
August The calmest doors: 78.9% seen within 4 hours — the year's best performance.
January The hardest month to keep the promise: 73.6% despite quiet corridors — flu season meets beds already full.
February The quietest month for arrivals (8% below average) — yet still no recovery in waiting times.

The counter-intuitive bit: crowds aren't the problem — winter is. The busiest summer months still hit ~77–79%, while quiet January delivers the worst waits. This may suggest the binding constraint is hospital capacity — staffed beds — which winter consumes, not visitor numbers.

04 · The biggest discoveries

Five things this data
will not let us forget.

1

The promise was kept once in eleven years.

In July 2015, 95.0% of patients were seen within four hours — the only month in this entire dataset that met the national standard. In the 128 months since, it hasn't happened once, even at the pandemic's quietest. This may suggest the target was already slipping out of reach before anything "broke" — and the pandemic then reset what "normal" means.

A quarter of all patients now wait longer than four hours.

Over the full eleven years, 79.4% of visits with recorded waits ended within four hours. That sounds tolerable — until you divide: roughly one missed promise every four visits, forever. Where you go decides how long you wait.

05 · What should we take away?

If you remember three things…

The fact
England's A&Es treated 261.8 million people in eleven years — and the share seen quickly fell from ~90% to ~75% and has never recovered.
The twist
The problem isn't crowds. Summer's record queues outperform winter's quiet ones; the missing ingredient is flow — staffed beds to move admitted patients into.
The human sum
Behind the averages: 6.5 million breached waits last year and 553,345 twelve-hour trolley waits. Every number on this page is a person who waited.

Honest limitations — read before quoting this page

  • Waiting-time detail starts in April 2017. Before that, NHS publications omitted per-provider breach counts, so all rates here cover April 2017 onward; totals cover the full eleven years.
  • Five months are incomplete in our copy — Jan 2016, Aug/Oct/Dec 2022 and Dec 2025 have little or no provider-level data, and January 2024 contains only one site. National figures for those months should be read with care.
  • "Within 4 hours" is measured against the total arrival-to-departure journey, including the wait after a decision to admit — which is exactly why trolley waits matter so much.
  • This measures waits, not outcomes. Nothing here says whether patients were healthier or sicker at the end — only how long they waited.
  • Sites open, close, merge and rename. 63 organisations changed names during these years; we track them by their permanent code, so history stays attached to the right place.

Where this comes from

The source

NHS England publishes monthly “A&E Attendances and Emergency Admissions” statistics under the Open Government Licence. Each file reports every provider's monthly counts — the raw material for everything on this page.

The journey

  • Monthly .xls files downloaded automatically
  • Messy two-row headers parsed; duplicates dropped
  • The national England row removed (it would double-count)
  • A known breach-definition quirk corrected (>4h vs <4h)
  • Each month reconciled against NHS national totals
  • One clean row per site per month: 28,212 of them

On this page

  • Every number queried directly from the cleaned warehouse table fct_ae_activity
  • Rates computed only over months where waits were published
  • No estimates, no projections, no invented values
  • Facts say “the data shows…”; readings say “this may suggest…”
